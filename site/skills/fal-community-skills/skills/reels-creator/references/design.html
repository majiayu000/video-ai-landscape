Design: free, never slop

Every video gets its own look. This page doesn't hand you a palette, a font or a motion style. It lists what reads as cheap, what makes a scene "cool", and a method that finds a look the user will pick.

1. Freedom

  • Derive the look from the subject and the audience, not from the last video. A science explainer, a cooking reel and a news recap should not share a palette or a font.
  • Mixing looks in one video is welcome when the reason is clear: for example, light pastel scenes with stroke boxes for structure, and black cinematic scenes with real footage for proof.
  • Brands use their own system: their real fonts, colours and marks, verified on the brand's own site (licensed faces only with files the user supplies). Never draw a logo by hand: use its official paths (textkit.mark()).
  • Any technique in §5 is available; none is required. Don't tie a video to a single animation style or design.

2. The text budget

The words on screen are the voice's shadow, not a second script. Too much text is the first thing that makes a scene look cheap.

  • One idea per scene. The headline, if there is one, is a single line of at most six words.
  • Labels name things (≤ 3 words each) and appear on the word that says them (word times from wordtimes.py). A label the voice never says is a candidate to cut.
  • No sentences on screen, except a quoted claim with its source.
  • Visible words at any moment:
  • 16:9: aim for ≤ 12, hard ceiling 20;
  • 9:16: aim for ≤ 8, ceiling 12;
  • captions and the one-line source footnote don't count.
  • Numbers may be big. A number the voice says can fill a third of the frame. That is often the best scene.
  • Every number gets a dated source line (who.int · Oct 2026), small, in one corner, set once per scene.
  • Text over a host clip never touches the face or the hair. On a 9:16 talking head, the plain area below the captions (often the shirt) is the clean ground.

3. Tells: the AI-slop look

Never ship: - coloured accent bars on a card's left or top edge (border-left / border-top), or "callout" boxes; - a grid of white or grey cards with hairline borders on a grey ground, the dashboard look. A card is fine when it is a real object (a widget, a phone, a ticket, a window on footage), not a container for bullet text; - uppercase, letter-spaced mono "eyebrow" labels over headings; - bento grids that exist to fill space, and three-icon feature rows; - gradient blobs, decorative glows, and glass with no light source; - text that fades and rises in, line after line, as the scene's only motion; - emoji or stock icons with mismatched strokes; - rolling odometer strips on every number (half digits show in paused frames); - floating corner badges, "NEW" stickers, pulsing dots; - template transitions: zoom blur, glitch, page curl, cube spin, a swipe wipe between every scene; - drop shadows on everything, and three different corner radii in one frame; - a centred title over three cards (a slide, not a scene); - labels off-centre in their boxes (the bar is ±2 px, measured by check.py); - flat clip-art drawings in two saturated colours, and full-bleed split colour blocks.

4. Cooler = the object explains

A scene is cool when the thing itself moves to make the point, so the voice needs no help. Ask of every scene: what is the one thing that moves, and what does its movement say?

Moves that work: - Footage that proves the number. A score on a chart flies into a window that opens on the footage it scored: this score is this video. The footage then docks into a card while the next fact arrives, so the result keeps its place. - A duration you watch. A stopwatch counts while a clip plays: a speed claim becomes a length of time, not a number you read. - The layout is the argument. A box spans only the columns it covers, so its width says what it does. - No half-empty frame. A box holds the middle alone, then slides aside when its partner draws on. - Handovers. Consecutive animation scenes hand over by a circle reveal of the next ground from the point of interest. After a host shot or b-roll, the scene just cuts in. - The reveal lives inside the incoming scene: its first frame shows the previous scene's last ground, and its own ground opens as a circle. - host_reel only hard-cuts, and the cut is invisible because the two frames match.

More directions that fit this rule: - a bar that is the thing it counts (stacked clips, coins, GPUs); - a map region lighting up as it's named; - a timeline that becomes the next scene's ground; - a price that physically shrinks next to the one it beats; - a UI the viewer knows (a terminal, a phone, a player) doing the real thing.

5. Techniques catalogue (techniques, not a style)

Borrow freely and adapt to the look: - Studio motion techniques: squircle morphs, colour reveals, flip faces, word-pop line launches, slanted band masks, velocity-matched zoom cuts, card tilt-and-rise, radial gauges, counters with centred bars, rack focus, and a physical object for one moment. - The measured house curve: arrivals leave at full speed and land softly (cubic-bezier 0.085, 0.648, 0, 1, 0.55–0.85 s); exits into a cut accelerate to the last frame. - Premium rhythm: one loud thing on a quiet ground, one idea per shot, a pace that breathes. - Words that perform their meaning ("cut" cuts, "slide" slides), used once, not on every word. - Real footage inside the design: frame sequences in rounded windows (textkit.seq), docked cards, masks that follow an object. - Generated plates: an H3 Max abstract plate or a FLUX 3 Max real-world shot as a scene's ground, with the design drawn on top.

6. Method

  1. Scene list first. Per scene: its line, its one idea, its one moving object, its ground. This is cheap, so show it with the script.
  2. Directions. Make 2–3 directions as stills on the same two scenes, picking the hardest ones, on one sheet with short labels (A, B, C, one line each). - The directions differ in look, not just colour. - Never re-propose a rejected direction.
  3. The user picks or mixes. Then make a short motion test of the same two scenes with the real voice (preview_mux.py) before building the rest.
  4. Build every scene. Send stills of each scene's key moments (check.mjs) before the full render.
  5. After any rejection, stills come first again. Never go straight to a full render.

7. Type

  • Faces: brand fonts first. Otherwise one display face with character and one text face, plus one mono only when it carries data.
  • Check licences: OFL faces (Geist, Inter, Space Grotesk…) are safe to load in the local workspace; licensed brand faces only with the user's files and OK.
  • Load fonts with @font-face in the page's fonts.css, verify them with assertFonts, and check glyph coverage with check.py.
  • Sizes: judge them on the final frame, at phone size.
  • At 1920 × 1080: body ≥ 34 px, labels ≥ 24 px, the source line ≥ 20 px.
  • At 1080 × 1920: body ≥ 44 px, labels ≥ 30 px.
  • Figures: tabular for anything that counts. Use the real minus, real quotes and real apostrophes when the face has them; check.py tells you when it doesn't.
  • Brand names keep their own case: avoid label styles that force uppercase.
