Story Breakdown — Deluxe compact: Short Story → Acts → Scenes → Clips

From five books (Cowgill, Phillips, Johnson, Gurskis, Giglio), for a pipeline where a clip = one continuous generated shot (4–8 s), a scene = clips in one place/time, an act = a movement. A story beat = one unit of change ≈ one scene; a shot beat = one attack/counter/pause/reaction inside a scene = one clip.

Foundations

  • Two engines: conflict (someone wants something and is having trouble getting it) and connection (stories also turn on moments of connection/disconnection — comedy ends in connection, tragedy in disconnection: test every ending's valence).
  • Phrase the dramatic question as a question; every scene serves it, the climax answers it.
  • Want must be specific to this plot (never "happiness"); the need is what the character couldn't tell you if asked; pursuing the want exposes the need, and endings often satisfy the need while the want is lost.
  • The atoms of story are discoveries and decisions (discovery → deliberation → decision → doing); a dramatic choice must be able to alter a life or the world.
  • Emotion is the story — vary it scene to scene; it flows across cuts.
  • Show, don't tell: run "How Do We Know?" on every fact; mute the film and the plot should still track. Character is action; deliberation gets a clip.
  • Voltage rule: the shorter the film, the less trauma it carries before melodrama. One main event per short; narrative cram kills — smaller is better.
  • Arc scales with runtime: no pole-to-pole transformation under ~10 min — moderate change seeded before scene one, or an unchanged protagonist who changes others.
  • Antagonist: hero of their own story, worthy opponent within this conflict; unity of opposites (mutually exclusive objectives). Confidant = the naturalistic outlet for interiority (prefer over VO). Cast: 2–3 min film ≈ two characters.

Size, then map beats (two passes)

  • Sizes (Gurskis): 2–4 min = 1–2 scenes, one crisis; 7–12 min ≈ 5–8 scenes; 20–25 min ≈ 12–15; 30+ ≈ 20–30. No B-plot in short-shorts or conventional shorts (≤12 min). Compress story time toward hours; add a time lock or condition lock. Pipeline: one story beat = one scene of 2–6 clips (≥3 shot beats); board under ~100 clips — cut named beats, never thin everything.
  • Pass 1 (step outline): number every story beat (~10–30) with its source passage, scanning for conflict AND connection beats; tag each +/− (tags must alternate); mark inciting incident, midpoint, crisis, climax, resolution, plants/payoffs, locks; test the chain reads "A, THEREFORE B, BUT C" — never "and then"; check each major event has a preparation beat before and reaction beat after; flag unfilmable passages.
  • Pass 2 (scene outline): per scene list every minor beat — that list becomes the clips.
  • Verify after drafting: every story beat → ≥1 clip or consciously cut with a reason; bridge scenes (pure transitions) go first.

Acts

  • Setup: setting, tone, protagonist hint, and the inciting incident ending the setup (≈ quarter mark at latest, or pre-story). Three scenes max to open; under 15 min, conflict by scene two. Plant now what the ending pays off.
  • Middle: cause and effect; state the plan in one plain line; vary opposition (person, physical, inner, mystic/fate/chance/moral); alternate wins and losses; midpoint reversal or pseudo-solution; the middle is the promise of the premise and ends at the worst moment. Pick the knowledge dial per scene: mystery (audience knows less), suspense (same), dramatic irony (more — the short's cheapest tension).
  • Ending: climax external — protagonist present, personally staked, a visible event, never internal. Shorts end on twist (true, recontextualizing — not cheap), irony, or revelation; open endings fine if chosen; no circular endings, no resolution-by-death. Resolution = one scene. Audiences forgive almost anything at the beginning and almost nothing at the end.

Scenes

  • Any location change or time jump = new scene. Every scene turns; one point, statable in one sentence; nothing changed → cut.
  • Each scene has its own scene-protagonist, scene-objective, and opposition. The reliable engine is the boxing match: one wants what the other won't give — tie the want to a physical object.
  • Enter late (action underway), leave early; no greetings or drive-ups; prefer face-to-face in one location over phone scenes unless the premise demands one. End on a hook answered in a later scene. Build least → most important; a scene may complete by implication in the next.
  • Two pages preferred, three max; vary mood scene to scene.

Clips

  • One shot beat = one clip, one camera idea; ≥3 per scene; each new paragraph of action ≈ one shot. Never a whole scene in one clip; "fewer, denser" ≠ dropping beats.
  • Meaning lives between clips (the Kuleshov/Eisenstein principle): order deliberately; a reaction shot after a reveal writes the emotion wordlessly. A held silent reaction is often the strongest beat.
  • Vary shot size; no identical adjacent framings of the same subject (reverse angles fine); give the frame three concrete setting details; business is characterization; the turn must be visible in a clip.
  • Prompts fully self-contained; never "same as before"; repeat prop/wardrobe/lighting/location anchors. Write the specific gesture, not the named emotion — no actor exists to interpret. No dialogue in video prompts unless the model renders speech. Image prompt = one still; first-frame vs reference chosen.
  • Montage = 2–4 single-image clips compressing time; never montage what deserves a dramatized scene. End scenes and acts on their strongest image.

Dialogue

  • Gate: a line advances plot, reveals character, carries needed info, sets tone, or serves theme — or is cut. Write dialogue last, after the visual story works.
  • One idea per speech; 1–2 lines; strongest word LAST (second-most first); break long speeches into exchanges; monologues only as motivated self-revelation.
  • Text simple, subtext complex; never write what an actor could play. Exception: the want may be stated plainly once (the plan/"I want" line); feelings and theme, never. Exposition is earned through friction, never briefed.
  • Distinct voice per character; techniques: signature phrase, indirectness, saying the opposite, echo line returning changed, Greek Chorus friend voicing doubts.
  • No names between intimates; nod/shake replace yes/no; the last line is what the audience carries out. Format NAME: line, exact known character names.

Voiceover

Job test + ladder: dramatize > confidant scene > VO > direct address. VO only for compression, irony against the image, a told frame, the epistolary bridge (letters in VO interlocking characters who never meet), poetic overlay, or binding vignettes. Never to restate the image, deliver "and then I realized," or patch structure. Convention (planning-only): NARRATOR: / NARRATOR (CONT'D):, never in characters; span text split across clips' dialogue; duration ≈ words ÷ 2.5, ~10–20 words/clip (≈4–8 s).

Prose conversion + failure modes

  • Interiority → reaction shot/business → confidant → VO (in that order). Summary → montage or cut the jump. Reported speech → the live scene. Stated feeling → the specific behavior. Backstory → one planted object; flashback only for revelation. What a character fails to do is filmable; the biggest beat can live off-screen in its reaction.
  • Watch for: late inciting incident; too few major beats; unarticulated conflict; mismatched opponents; hasty resolutions; voltage overload; narrative cram; episodic drift; circular endings; silent compression (antidote: the two-pass map, verified); internal climax; cross-referencing clips; caps ≤8/≤12/≤12 (split acts, state merges); invented entities; revision drift.

Shapes to steal

Life Lessons (locked pair, unchanged protagonist); The Lunch Date (near-wordless irony, recontextualizing twist); Owl Creek (jeopardy in images, twist-as-climax); PEEL (prop-driven standoff); Ray's (vignettes on ironic VO); The String Bean (wordless, 42 scenes ≈ 24 s — clip-scale precedent); Kosher (5 pages of single-point scenes); Lena's Spaghetti (epistolary VO); Miracle Fish (midpoint dramatic irony, climax of connection); Black Hole (3-minute premise-promise-snap).
